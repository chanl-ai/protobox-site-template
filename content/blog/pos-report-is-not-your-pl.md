Toast, Square, and Clover all produce a clean end-of-day sales summary, and it's tempting to treat that number as the top line of your P&L. It's close, but three gaps show up every single time a POS report gets used as a substitute for a real profit and loss statement.

## Comps and voids move sales, not cost

A comped birthday dessert reduces recorded sales, but the ingredients still left the walk-in. If comps run 2-3% of sales — normal for a hospitality-forward concept — your true food cost percentage is understated every time someone reads the POS summary at face value.

## Discounts on delivery platforms hide in the wrong place

A 20% off promo run through a delivery aggregator often nets out in the platform's payout report, not the POS. If your bookkeeping starts from POS sales and reconciles bank deposits separately, that promo cost can disappear into "processing fees" instead of showing up as a marketing or discount line where you'd actually see it.

## Tips and service charges are not revenue, even when the POS shows them that way

Distributed tips and mandatory service charges passed through to staff inflate the top-line sales number if they're not separated in the chart of accounts. A restaurant that looks like it's doing $52,000 a week might actually be doing $47,000 once $5,000 in pass-through service charges are pulled out — a material difference when you're calculating prime cost percentage against sales.

## What a real P&L does differently

A P&L built from reconciled books — POS data checked against bank deposits, vendor invoices, and payroll, with comps, discounts, and pass-through tips coded to their own lines — gives you a food cost and labor cost percentage you can actually act on. The POS report is a good first read of the day. It was never meant to be the only one.
