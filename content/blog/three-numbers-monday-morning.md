Most restaurant owners open Monday to a stack of things demanding attention — a broken walk-in fan, a no-call-no-show, a vendor invoice that doesn't match the delivery. None of those are the first thing to look at. Three numbers are, and in order: prime cost, covers per labor hour, and cash-on-hand. They take under ten minutes if last week's books closed on Friday.

| Number | Where it comes from | Act when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prime cost | Food + labor from the weekly close, ÷ the week's sales | Above 60% (full service) or 55% (counter) |
| Covers per labor hour | POS covers ÷ scheduled labor hours | Down 15% vs your 4-week average |
| Cash-on-hand | Operating accounts minus known autopays | Below 2 payroll cycles + 1 month rent |

## What does prime cost tell you on a Monday?

Prime cost — food plus labor as a share of sales — is the single number that says whether last week made money, and on Monday it's still fresh enough to explain. If the weekly close puts it above 60% for full service, the question is which half moved: food (a vendor reprice, waste, a theft pattern) or labor (overscheduling against soft sales). One week high is a question; two consecutive weeks high is a decision.

The trap is using the POS theoretical instead of invoices. The POS knows what should have left the kitchen at recipe cost; only vendor invoices know what you actually paid. The gap between the two is usually 2–4 points, and it is exactly the part you can act on.

## Why track covers per labor hour instead of labor percent?

Labor percent hides the mechanism: it moves when sales move, even if scheduling was perfect. Covers per labor hour isolates the thing a manager controls — how much labor was scheduled against how much business showed up. When it drops 15% against your own four-week average, the schedule was built for a week that didn't happen, and this week's schedule, still unposted on Monday morning, is where the correction lands.

It also travels across concepts badly, so never benchmark against another restaurant's number. Benchmark against your own trailing month, per daypart if service and prep are separated.

## How much cash-on-hand is actually safe?

Two payroll cycles plus a month of rent, minus anything that autopays this week. Cash is the number that turns the other two from analysis into constraint: high prime cost with strong cash is a project to fix over a month; high prime cost with thin cash is a decision to make today, on the produce order, this week's schedule, and whether the owner takes a draw.

Check it last, because the first two explain where next week's cash is going. A P&L that looks fine while the account drains almost always means the gap is sitting in vendor terms — the food you sold this week was bought on last month's prices and next month's due dates.

## What has to be true for the ten minutes to work?

One thing: a weekly close. Books reconciled every Friday — POS to bank to vendor invoices — are what turn Monday from archaeology into steering. Without the close, all three numbers exist only as guesses, and the ten-minute check quietly becomes an hour of spreadsheet spelunking that doesn't happen after the second week.

That's the whole argument for weekly bookkeeping in one sentence: it isn't about cleaner records, it's about making Monday's ten minutes possible.
