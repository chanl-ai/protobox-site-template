Prime cost worksheets tend to fail one of two ways: they're either a single number pulled from a POS dashboard with no context, or they're a 12-tab spreadsheet nobody updates past week three. The version that actually gets used weekly has four columns and takes about fifteen minutes once the books are closed for the week.

## The four columns

**Net sales.** Total sales minus comps, voids, and pass-through tips or service charges — not the raw POS total.

**Cost of goods sold.** Beginning inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory, for the week. If you're not doing a weekly inventory count, use purchases alone and flag the number as directional rather than exact — it's still more useful than a monthly average.

**Total labor cost.** Gross wages plus payroll taxes and workers' comp allocated to the week, for both front and back of house.

**Prime cost percentage.** (Cost of goods sold + total labor cost) divided by net sales.

## Why weekly beats monthly

A monthly prime cost number averages away the week that actually had a problem. If week two ran 71% because a delivery came in short and got covered with a rush order at retail pricing, a monthly average might smooth that down to a comfortable-looking 64% — and the underlying vendor problem never gets flagged.

## What to do when it moves

A one-week spike is worth a look, not a panic — check for a one-time event like a spoiled delivery or a comped private event. Two consecutive weeks above your target range is worth pulling the vendor invoices and the schedule for that period specifically. Three weeks running means something structural changed — a new vendor contract, a menu price that didn't keep pace with cost increases, or a schedule built for volume that isn't showing up anymore — and it needs a decision, not another week of watching.

Track it every week, in the same four columns, and the worksheet does the job a much more complicated report usually fails at: telling you which week to actually worry about.
